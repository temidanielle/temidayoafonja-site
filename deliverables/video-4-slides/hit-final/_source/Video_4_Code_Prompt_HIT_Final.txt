BEGIN APPROVED VIDEO 4 SCRIPT

A senior colleague once called me a cat with nine lives.

She meant my career kept moving into work that looked unrelated.

When I explained it as a list of jobs, the path sounded more disconnected than it was.

The career wasn’t the problem. The explanation was.

Here are three steps to show what traveled with you—without pretending every move was planned.

[SLIDE: Career Path]

I’m Temidayo Afonja. I help experienced professionals understand what they can carry across roles, functions, employers and industries so they can make career pivots and internal moves without starting from zero.

My own career has crossed functions and industries that looked very different on paper.

The slide gives you the chronology, so I am not going to read every chapter back to you.

What matters is what I eventually learned to hear underneath it.

The titles changed.

The work did not stay identical.

Some knowledge belonged to the context I was leaving, and every move required real learning.

But certain forms of judgment kept showing up again.

That is what made the career easier to explain.

[SLIDE: Chronology / Portability]

A chronology tells people where you have been.

A portability explanation tells them what traveled with you.

That distinction matters because when someone asks, “Walk me through your background,” most of us give them a list.

I worked here.

Then I moved there.

Then I changed functions.

Then I entered another industry.

The listener receives the sequence, but they still have to build the meaning.

If your path is nonlinear, do not make them do all of the integration for you.

Give them enough chronology to orient them.

Then explain what your work repeatedly required you to become able to do.

There are three parts.

[SLIDE: 1 — Name the Chapters Briefly]

First, name the chapters briefly.

The goal is orientation, not defense.

You might say:

“My career began in accounting and audit, moved into cybersecurity, and later expanded into people strategy and enterprise transformation.”

That is enough to establish the shape of the path.

You do not need to explain every title, every employer or every decision.

And if a real constraint changed the direction, name it plainly.

For me, the 2008 financial crisis is relevant context.

I graduated into that market, so the first turn in my career was not part of a carefully designed portfolio plan.

Context can explain a transition without becoming an apology.

That is important because a coherent career story does not require you to pretend that every move was strategic.

It requires you to tell the truth about what happened and then interpret what the work actually built.

[SLIDE: 2 — Find the Repeated Work]

The second part is to find the repeated work beneath the titles.

This is where I would stop asking only:

“What skills did I use in each job?”

Instead ask:

“What kind of situation did people repeatedly trust me to enter?”

“What did I have to notice?”

“What did I have to make understandable?”

“What was I trusted to build, influence or decide?”

[SLIDE: Look Beneath the Nouns]

Look beneath the nouns.

Job titles.

Company language.

Industry vocabulary.

Those things can make different chapters sound farther apart than they really are.

Verbs can show you the recurring work.

When I looked across my own career, recurring verbs included examine, translate, connect and build.

Earlier work trained me to follow evidence and understand how systems were operating.

Later work required me to translate complexity across people who did not use the same language and create enough structure for better decisions.

That does not mean accounting, cybersecurity and people strategy are the same profession.

They are not.

And I would not force a connection where the evidence does not support one.

The point is narrower.

Some of the judgment formed in one chapter remained useful when the context changed.

Your verbs might be diagnose, stabilize, negotiate, teach, simplify, design or align.

But each verb needs evidence.

A portable capability is not a word you like.

It is something your work repeatedly required you to demonstrate.

[SLIDE: 3 — Explain the Direction]

The third part is to explain why the direction you want next follows from what you have already built.

The goal is not to prove that the next move was inevitable.

It is to make the connection understandable.

A simple three-sentence structure can help.

[SLIDE: Three-Sentence Structure]

“My career has moved across…”

“Across those chapters, I kept being asked to…”

“That is why I am now focused on…”

This is an editing frame, not something you need to memorize word for word.

For example:

“My career has moved across accounting, cybersecurity, people strategy and enterprise transformation.

Across those chapters, I kept entering work where evidence had to be interpreted, complexity had to become understandable, and people and systems needed enough structure for a decision.

That is what now shapes my work in career portability and Capability Formation.”

Notice what that explanation does not say.

It does not say every chapter was the same.

It does not say every move was planned.

And it does not claim that everything I learned transferred automatically.

It shows why the next direction is connected to work I had already demonstrated.

[SLIDE: Do Not Invent a Perfect Plan]

There is an honesty test here.

Coherence is not the same as claiming that every move was strategic.

Some moves respond to a market.

Some happen because of caregiving, health, compensation, restructuring or an opportunity you could not have predicted.

Some are experiments.

Some clarify what you do not want.

If one chapter does not fit a clean progression, do not force it.

Explain what made the move necessary or worth trying.

Explain what became clearer.

And explain what, if anything, you carried forward.

A truthful career explanation can include interruption, redirection and relearning.

The goal is not to make your career look linear.

The goal is to make your formation legible.

[SLIDE: Explanation Test]

Before you use your explanation in an interview, networking conversation, LinkedIn profile or internal-move discussion, test it with three questions.

Can a stranger hear why the next move follows?

Does the explanation show ability—not only interest?

And is there evidence behind every important verb?

If you say you are strategic, where is the evidence?

If you say you build alignment, what did you actually align?

If you say you translate complexity, when did that change a decision or make action possible?

That is what keeps a career story from becoming branding language.

The explanation should be supported by work you can point to.

Write down three or four major career chapters.

Under each one, capture the problem you had to understand, the judgment people trusted you to carry, and one piece of permitted evidence showing what changed.

Then circle the verbs that repeat.

Use those verbs to draft your three sentences:

where the career moved;

what you repeatedly became able to do;

and why the next direction follows.

If the career still sounds disconnected after that, the problem may not be the path.

You may simply need better evidence behind the connection you are trying to make.

[SLIDE: Keep the Proof]

And that is where Keep the Proof fits.

If your career makes sense but the examples are hard to retrieve, Keep the Proof is a 60-minute career evidence system for capturing your work, your evidence and what that work shows while the context is still clear.

It is not a tool for inventing a stronger story.

It helps you preserve the proof behind the story you can actually support.

Use only your own recollection and information you are permitted to retain.

Do not take confidential, proprietary, customer, employee or employer-owned material.

You can find Keep the Proof at temidayoafonja.com/keep-the-proof.

[SLIDE: Watch Next]

And once you can explain what has traveled with you, the next question may be whether you actually need a different employer.

In the next video, I will give you three questions to test whether an internal move could give you the growth you need.

Watch Should I Make an Internal Move? 3 Questions to Decide next.

Your career does not have to look linear to make sense.

But you do have to make the continuity visible.
