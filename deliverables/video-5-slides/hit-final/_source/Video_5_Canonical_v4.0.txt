BEGIN APPROVED VIDEO 5 v4.0 SCRIPT

You may not need to leave your company.

You may need access to work your current role is not giving you yet.

Let me show you what this looked like for me.

About six months after I returned from maternity leave in one chapter of my career, my scope expanded beyond the original box of the role.

The important part was not that I had more work.

I was being trusted with different work.

That changed how I think about internal moves, and I want to help you test yours the same way.

Before you say yes, ask three questions: will the work change, will your judgment expand, and will the evidence travel?

[SLIDE: Core Distinction]

On this channel, I help experienced professionals understand what their work is building in them, what they can carry into a different context and what they may still need to learn.

And I want to help you do the same here.

Sometimes leaving is the right decision.

But you do not always have to change employers to reach work that expands you.

An internal move can give you access to a different problem, function, customer, system or level of responsibility while letting you use context and credibility you have already built.

That is one of the real advantages of moving internally.

You are not beginning with zero context.

But I also want you to look at the other side of the decision.

An internal move is not only a decision you make.

It is also a decision about what your organization is actually willing to trust you to do next.

That matters to you because the same leaders who know your strengths may still see you through the work you already perform.

You can be deeply valued in your current role and still find that people struggle to picture you somewhere else.

So I do not want you to ask only:

Do I want this role?

I want you to ask:

What is this organization actually willing to let me learn, decide and own?

[SLIDE: The Three Questions]

Here are the three questions I want you to use.

Will the work change?

Will your judgment expand?

And will the evidence travel?

The point is not simply whether you moved.

The point is whether the move increased what you can carry afterward.

[SLIDE: 1 — Will the Work Change?]

Start with the first question:

Will the work change?

Do not start with the title.

Picture an ordinary Monday in the new role.

What would actually be different?

What problems would you be close enough to understand?

What systems would become visible to you?

Which stakeholders would you have to influence?

What context would you learn that you cannot see from where you sit today?

[SLIDE: Access Test]

I would look for at least some change in four places:

New problems.

New systems.

New stakeholders.

New context.

You do not need everything to be unfamiliar.

Part of the advantage of moving internally is that you already know some things.

You may understand the culture, the decision process, the history or the relationships.

That context can help you learn the new work faster.

But familiarity can also make a weak move look stronger than it is.

You could move to another team and discover that the nouns changed but the problems did not.

You could receive a bigger title and keep solving the same problems with the same tools.

And you may run into another issue that is easy to miss.

The problem may not be your capability.

It may be whether people can see you beyond the work they already know you for.

You may find that people keep interpreting you through the role you already perform.

So when you have an internal conversation, do not ask only:

Is there an opening?

Ask:

What would I actually own in the first six months?

What would I have to learn that I do not already know?

What part of the organization would I understand differently because I held this role?

And if people are not yet ready to trust you with the move, ask:

What evidence would change that?

That question matters because it turns a vague “not yet” into something you can examine.

Listen for concrete work and concrete signals.

If someone tells you the role is “strategic,” “visible” or “high impact,” keep going.

Those words do not tell you what you will become able to do.

And there is an important limit here.

A move that does not change the work may still be right for you.

Maybe the pay is better.

Maybe the flexibility matters.

Maybe the manager is better.

Maybe you need stability right now.

Those are legitimate reasons.

I just do not want you to confuse a useful move with a developmental one.

[SLIDE: 2 — Will Your Judgment Expand?]

Now ask the second question:

Will your judgment expand?

This is where I want you to be careful with the phrase “more responsibility.”

[SLIDE: More Tasks / More Judgment]

More tasks can mean volume, coordination and work that no longer has a clear owner.

More judgment means interpretation, tradeoffs and consequence.

Your organization may give you more because you are dependable.

More projects.

More people to coordinate.

More work that somebody needs you to absorb.

That can make you busier without making the work more developmental.

Your judgment is expanding when you have to work with incomplete information, weigh competing interests, recommend a direction, influence people who do not want the same thing, or own what happens after a decision.

So here is what I would ask you:

What will you be expected to notice that you are not expected to notice now?

Which decisions will you make, shape or defend?

What outcome will still be yours when the instructions are incomplete?

A report can be a task.

Deciding which pattern in the report matters, explaining the risk and recommending what happens next requires more judgment.

Scheduling a cross-functional meeting can be a task.

Creating enough shared understanding for several functions to make a decision is different work.

I am not saying the operational task does not matter.

I am asking what the work is forming in you.

And I want you to clarify authority.

If the role makes you accountable for an outcome but gives you very little ability to influence the decisions that produce it, you may be getting exposure without real ownership.

This is also where the organization’s role matters.

A stretch opportunity becomes developmental when you are trusted with more judgment, not simply handed more volume.

So ask the hiring manager:

Which decisions belong to this role?

Where will I make a recommendation rather than only provide information?

What will I be trusted to carry six months from now that I am not trusted to carry today?

If the clearest answer is:

“You’ll have more to manage,”

keep investigating.

[SLIDE: 3 — Will the Evidence Travel?]

Then ask the third question:

Will the evidence travel?

This one matters because your credibility inside the company may not automatically make sense outside it.

People around you may already know that you are the person who can get something done.

Another employer will not have that context.

They will need to understand what you actually did.

[SLIDE: Result / Judgment / Range]

I want you to look for three kinds of evidence.

Result:

What changed?

Judgment:

What did you notice or decide?

Range:

What new context can you now handle?

A strong internal move should leave you with something more useful than:

“I worked with a more senior team.”

I want you to be able to say:

I entered a new context.

I was trusted to make this kind of judgment.

And here is what changed because of the work.

That is evidence somebody outside your company can understand.

Before you accept the opportunity, ask how success will be measured.

What would a strong first year allow you to point to?

Which outcomes would you own rather than only support?

And if you can only explain the opportunity using internal acronyms, relationships and visibility, translate it before you decide.

If you cannot explain what the role could prove, you may not yet understand what it will build.

[SLIDE: Decision Read]

Now I want you to put the three answers together.

If you have three yeses, you have a strong formation case.

The work changes.

Your judgment expands.

The evidence can travel.

That does not automatically mean you should take the role.

You still need to think about the manager, compensation, workload, stability and the rest of your life.

But the developmental case is strong.

If you have two yeses, investigate the missing dimension.

Maybe the work is new and the evidence will be visible, but the decision authority is vague.

Now you have something concrete to ask about.

What will I own?

Which recommendations will be mine?

What would have to happen for the scope to expand?

If you have zero or one yes, you may be moving without adding very much to what you can carry next.

Again, that does not automatically make the move wrong.

A better manager, more flexibility, higher pay, stronger benefits or stability may make it exactly the right choice for you.

Just name the trade accurately.

You do not have to call every useful move growth.

[SLIDE: Conversation Prompts]

Before your next internal conversation, I want you to get answers to three things.

What problems will I own in the first six months?

Which decisions belong to this role?

How will success be measured?

Those questions will tell you much more than the title.

And they will also tell you something about the organization.

Is this opportunity actually designed to give you access to new work, clearer authority and visible outcomes?

Because you can be capable and still end up in a poorly designed role.

You may be given accountability without enough authority.

You may be asked to absorb work instead of being developed into different work.

Or you may find that people still see you only through the role you already perform.

I am not telling you to assume the organization is wrong and you are right.

I am telling you to get enough evidence before you call the opportunity growth.

And you can use these same three questions on an external opportunity.

The external role does not win simply because it is outside.

The internal role does not win simply because it is familiar.

I want you to compare which option gives you meaningful access to different work, stronger judgment and clearer evidence at a cost you can responsibly carry.

That is a much better comparison than simply:

Stay or go?

There are also situations where an internal move will not solve the problem you are facing.

The organization may not contain the work you need.

The role may offer accountability without authority.

Or the environment itself may be unhealthy.

If your health or safety is at risk, or you are dealing with harassment or discrimination, you do not need to optimize an internal-mobility strategy before protecting yourself and getting appropriate support.

And your career decision sits inside your actual life.

Pay, benefits, caregiving, location, immigration status, energy and timing can all legitimately change the answer.

These three questions help you read the work.

They do not erase the rest of your life.

[SLIDE: Career Decision Evidence Check]

Before your next internal conversation, write one sentence under each question.

What different work will I enter?

What judgment will I be trusted to carry?

What evidence could I explain a year from now?

If you are actively deciding whether to stay, move internally or leave, I made the Career Decision Evidence Check to help you organize the evidence behind that choice.

You can find it at temidayoafonja.com/career-decisions.

[SLIDE: Watch Next]

And I want to leave you with one warning underneath all of this.

More responsibility can look like growth when it is really just more work.

That is what I want to help you test next.

Watch Are You Growing—or Just Being Given More Work?

You may not need to leave.

But your next move should increase what you can carry.
