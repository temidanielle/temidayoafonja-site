Should I Make an Internal Move? 3 Questions to Decide
Video 5  ·  Teleprompter script with slide markers

You may not need to leave your company.

You may need access to work the company has not trusted you with yet.

About six months after I returned from maternity leave in one chapter of my career, my scope expanded beyond the original box of the role.

What mattered was not simply that I had more work.

I was being trusted with different work.

That changed how I think about internal mobility.

Before you accept an internal move, ask three questions: will the work change, will your judgment expand, and will the evidence travel?


SLIDE  —  Core Distinction

I’m Temidayo Afonja. I help experienced professionals understand what their work has built in them, what can travel and what the next context will require from them.

Sometimes leaving is the right decision.

But changing employers is not the only way to reach work that expands you.

An internal move can give you access to a different problem, function, customer, system or level of responsibility while letting you use context and credibility you have already built.

That is one of the real advantages of moving internally.

You are not beginning with zero context.

But there is another side to it.

An internal move is not only a decision you make.

It is also a decision the organization is making about what it is willing to trust you to do next.

That matters because the same people who know your strengths may also know you too well inside one box.

They may value you deeply in your current role and still struggle to picture you somewhere else.

So the question is not simply:

Do I want this role?

It is also:

What is this organization actually willing to let me learn, decide and own?


SLIDE  —  The Three Questions

Use three questions to test that.

Will the work change?

Will your judgment expand?

And will the evidence travel?

The real question is not simply whether you moved.

It is whether the move increased what you can carry afterward.


SLIDE  —  1 — Will the Work Change?

The first question is:

Will the work change?

Do not start with the title.

Ask what will actually be different on an ordinary Monday.

What problems will you be close enough to understand?

What systems will become visible?

Which stakeholders will you have to influence?

What context will you learn that you cannot see from your current seat?


SLIDE  —  Access Test

A meaningful internal move should usually change at least some of these:

New problems.

New systems.

New stakeholders.

New context.

That does not mean everything has to be unfamiliar.

Part of the advantage of moving internally is that some things are already known.

You may understand the culture, the decision process, the history or the relationships.

That existing context can become leverage while you learn something new.

But familiarity can also hide a weak move.

You can move to another team and discover that the nouns changed but the problems did not.

You can receive a bigger title and keep solving the same problems with the same tools.

And sometimes the organizational problem is not your capability.

It is recognition.

People may continue interpreting you through the work they already know you for.

That is why an internal conversation should not only ask:

“Is there an opening?”

Ask what would have to become true for people to trust you with the next kind of work.

What are the most important problems this person will own in the first six months?

What will I have to learn that I do not already know?

What part of the organization will I understand differently because I held this role?

And if you are not yet seen as credible for the move:

What evidence would change that?

Listen for concrete work and concrete signals.

“Strategic,” “visible” and “high impact” sound attractive.

They do not tell you what you will become able to do.

A move that does not change the work may still be useful for pay, flexibility, stability or manager fit.

Just do not automatically call that development.


SLIDE  —  2 — Will Your Judgment Expand?

The second question is:

Will your judgment expand?

This is where more responsibility can be misleading.


SLIDE  —  More Tasks / More Judgment

More tasks can mean volume, coordination and absorption.

More judgment means interpretation, tradeoffs and consequence.

An organization may give you more because you are dependable.

More projects.

More people to coordinate.

More work that no longer has a clear owner.

That can make you busier without making the work more developmental.

Judgment expands when you have to work with incomplete information, weigh competing interests, recommend a direction, influence people who do not want the same thing, or own what happens after a decision.

So ask:

What will I be expected to notice that I am not expected to notice now?

Which decisions will I make, shape or defend?

And what outcome will still be mine when the instructions are incomplete?

A report can be a task.

Deciding which pattern in the report matters, explaining the risk and recommending what happens next requires more judgment.

Scheduling a cross-functional meeting is a task.

Creating enough shared understanding for several functions to make a decision is different work.

The distinction is not whether the operational task matters.

It is what the work is forming in you.

And clarify authority.

If the role gives you accountability for an outcome but very little ability to influence the decisions that produce it, you may be receiving exposure without real ownership.

From the organization’s side, that distinction matters too.

A stretch opportunity is developmental when the person is trusted with more judgment, not simply handed more volume.

So ask the hiring manager:

Which decisions belong to this role?

Where will I make a recommendation rather than only provide information?

What will I be trusted to carry six months from now that I am not trusted to carry today?

If the only answer is:

“You’ll have more to manage,”

keep investigating.


SLIDE  —  3 — Will the Evidence Travel?

The third question is:

Will the evidence travel?

Internal credibility can become very powerful inside one company and surprisingly difficult to explain outside it.

People may know you are the person who can make something happen.

But another employer still needs to understand what you actually did.


SLIDE  —  Result / Judgment / Range

Look for three forms of evidence.

Result: what changed?

Judgment: what did you notice or decide?

Range: what new context can you now handle?

A strong internal move should leave you with something more useful than:

“I worked with a more senior team.”

You want to be able to say:

I entered a new context.

I was trusted to make this kind of judgment.

And here is what changed because of the work.

That is evidence another context can understand.

Before you accept the opportunity, ask how success will be measured.

What would a strong first year allow you to point to?

Which outcomes would you own rather than only support?

If the opportunity can only be explained through internal acronyms, relationships and visibility, translate it before you decide.

If you cannot explain what the role could prove, you may not yet understand what it will build.


SLIDE  —  Decision Read

Now put the three answers together.

Three yeses:

Strong formation case.

Different work.

Expanded judgment.

Portable evidence.

That does not make the role automatically right.

You still need to evaluate the manager, compensation, workload, stability and the rest of your life.

But the developmental case is strong.

Two yeses:

Investigate the missing dimension.

Maybe the work is new and the evidence will be visible, but decision authority is vague.

That gives you something concrete to negotiate.

What will I own?

Which recommendations will be mine?

What would have to happen for the scope to expand?

Zero or one yes:

Movement may be happening without much formation.

That does not mean the move is wrong.

A better manager, more flexibility, higher pay, stronger benefits or stability can legitimately make it the right decision.

Just name the trade accurately.

Do not call every useful move growth.


SLIDE  —  Conversation Prompts

Before your next internal conversation, get answers to three things.

What problems will I own in the first six months?

Which decisions belong to this role?

How will success be measured?

Those questions will usually tell you far more than the title.

And notice what they also tell you about the organization.

They tell you whether the opportunity is actually designed to give somebody access to new work, clearer authority and visible outcomes.

That matters because an internal move can fail even when the person is capable.

The role may be poorly designed.

The authority may not match the accountability.

The organization may want somebody to absorb work rather than develop into different work.

Or people may continue seeing you only through the role you already perform.

That is not a reason to assume the organization is wrong and you are right.

It is a reason to get more evidence before you call the opportunity growth.

You can also use exactly the same three questions on an external opportunity.

The external role does not win simply because it is outside.

The internal role does not win simply because it is familiar.

Ask which option gives you meaningful access to different work, stronger judgment and clearer evidence at a cost you can responsibly carry.

That is a better comparison than simply:

Stay or go?

There are also situations where an internal move will not solve the problem.

The organization may not contain the work you need.

The new position may offer accountability without authority.

Or the environment itself may be unhealthy.

If your health or safety is at risk, or you are dealing with harassment or discrimination, you do not need to optimize an internal-mobility strategy before protecting yourself and getting appropriate support.

And career decisions sit inside real lives.

Pay, benefits, caregiving, location, immigration status, energy and timing can all legitimately change the answer.

The three questions help you read the work.

They do not erase the rest of the decision.

What judgment will I be trusted to carry?
