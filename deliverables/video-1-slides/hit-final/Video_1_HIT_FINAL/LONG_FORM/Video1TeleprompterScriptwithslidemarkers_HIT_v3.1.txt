How I Changed Jobs Without Starting My Career Over
Video 1  ·  Teleprompter script with slide markers

Changing jobs can make years of experience feel as though they belong to the place you’re leaving.

But a new context does not make you new to everything.

Over roughly eighteen years, my career has crossed very different functions and industries, so I had to learn what actually travels.

I’ll show you three things: how to look underneath your title, explain what your work changed, and keep the evidence before you need it.


SLIDE  —  Title

I’m Temidayo Afonja. I help experienced professionals understand what they can carry across roles, functions, employers and industries, so they can make career pivots and internal moves without starting from zero.

And I want to be clear about the promise.

Not everything transfers.

Some knowledge belongs to the company, industry, regulation or relationships around the work. A move can require real relearning.

But being new to a context is not the same as being new to every underlying problem.


SLIDE  —  My Career Path

The slide shows the broad chapters of my career, so I am not going to walk you through every title.

The first turn was not part of a perfect plan. I graduated with an accounting degree in December 2008, during the financial crisis. Later, the work crossed functions and industries with different language and real learning curves.

Some knowledge belonged to the setting I was leaving.

But the earlier chapters had also trained me to follow evidence, look for what a system was not revealing, translate complexity and create enough structure for a better decision.

I did not carry every task forward.

I carried forms of judgment.

So instead of only asking, “Does my old title match the new one?”, I learned to ask, “What has my work made me able to do, and where else could that be useful?”

Three practices helped me answer that.


SLIDE  —  01 — Look Underneath the Title

The first was to look underneath the title.


SLIDE  —  Move One

A title tells someone where the work sat.

It does not fully tell them what the work trained you to notice, decide or solve.

Two people can hold the same title and leave with very different judgment because they faced different problems, constraints and consequences.

The questions on screen are the ones I use.

What problems do people repeatedly trust me to solve?

What decisions can I now make with better judgment?

And what could I do in another setting because of what I learned here?

When I applied those questions to my own career, the nouns looked far apart.

Accounting. Cybersecurity. People strategy.

But some of the recurring work involved following evidence, finding what was missing from the obvious story, translating complexity and creating structure for a decision.

Those are not titles. They are forms of work and judgment.

And each one needs examples.

So take one title from your career and put it aside for a moment.

Write down three specific situations where people relied on your judgment. Then look for the recurring verb.

Were you diagnosing, stabilizing, translating, designing, aligning or building?

That is where portable capability often becomes visible.

But capability can travel while context still has to be learned.

You may know how to diagnose a system and still need to learn a new industry’s language, rules, stakeholders or risks.

That is not starting from zero.

That is carrying something proven into a real learning curve.


SLIDE  —  02 — Explain What the Work Changed

The second practice was to explain what the work changed.


SLIDE  —  Move Two

Many experienced professionals describe projects in language that only makes sense to people who were already there.

“I led an onboarding redesign.”

That is true.

But it names the activity, not the difference it made.

A more useful version is:

“I led an onboarding redesign with my team, and one measure of how well new hires felt integrated moved from 47 to 75.”

That sentence is not better simply because it includes a number.

It is better because an unfamiliar person can hear what I led and what changed.

Ask yourself:

What changed because I was there?

What became clearer, faster, safer, more consistent or more possible?

And what can I support without exaggerating?

The aim is not to make your work sound bigger.

It is to make the value easier to understand outside the original context.


SLIDE  —  One Result — 47 to 75

One measure of how well new hires felt integrated moved from 47 to 75 after the onboarding redesign I led with my team.

I want to be precise.

It was one measure of new-hire integration, not a claim about everything the redesign affected.

And it was team-based work that I led, not something I did alone.

That precision makes the evidence more credible, not less.

A number without context can mislead. An internal project name without a result tells too little.

Portable evidence connects the situation, your contribution and what changed.


SLIDE  —  03 — Keep Evidence Before You Need It

The third practice was to keep evidence before I needed it.


SLIDE  —  Move Three

Most of us wait until we need a résumé, an interview story or a promotion case.

By then, we may remember the project name but not the starting condition, the decision we made, exactly what changed or what part of the work was ours.

So create a permitted, high-level record in your own words.

Nothing confidential.

Nothing employer-owned.

The four lines on screen are enough:

The situation.

My role.

What changed.

And what this shows.

The slide uses the same onboarding example.

Notice the final line: what this shows.

It should be the narrowest capability the evidence can honestly support.

In this case, I can say that I have evidence of diagnosing a weak point in an employee experience and leading a redesign that improved one measure of integration.

I should not turn that into a claim that I can transform every employee experience problem.

Your record does not need the employer’s files.

It needs enough permitted context for you to reconstruct your own contribution accurately later.

The project name is easy to remember.

The evidence is what makes the experience useful later.


SLIDE  —  Three Things I Learned to Do

So those are the three practices that helped me carry experience forward.

Look underneath the title.

Explain what the work changed.

Keep evidence before you need it.

They helped me avoid two mistakes: assuming that everything transfers, and assuming that nothing does.

The first can make you overconfident about the learning curve.

The second can make you erase years of formation.

A more accurate position is:

I know what I can carry.

I know what evidence supports it.

And I know what I still need to learn.


SLIDE  —  Before Your Next Move

Before your next move, answer these three questions.

What can I solve now that I could not solve two years ago?

What result can I describe in language another employer would understand?

And what could I still do if the title, employer or industry changed?

Pause here if you want to write them down.

If your answers are specific, you may have more portable experience than your title reveals.

If they are vague, that does not automatically mean the capability is not there.

It may mean you need better evidence, more varied practice or a deliberate bridge before the move.

The goal is not to prove that you will never need to start anything new.

It is to avoid starting over where you already have evidence.
