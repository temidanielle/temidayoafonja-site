# WHAT REALLY TRANSFERS?
### A structured comparison of 28 senior individual-contributor program/project delivery postings across healthcare, financial services, and technology

Research compiled for a Capability Formation video. Collection date for every posting: September 10, 2026. This is a structured reading of what these specific employers wrote, not a labor-market study. See Section 12 for what that means.

---

## SECTION 1: EXECUTIVE FINDING

The evidence is genuinely mixed, and the mixed result is itself the most consequential finding: within this sample, the *management method* of senior IC delivery work travels almost everywhere, while the *thing being managed* almost never does, and employers are inconsistent about which of the two they gate on.

Three patterns carry the most weight.

First, a common spine of delivery work recurs in all three industries and is described in nearly interchangeable language: own a plan end to end, hold scope/schedule/budget, run a risk register, manage cross-functional stakeholders without direct authority, and report status upward to executives. This appears in some form in all 28 retained postings. On the written page, this spine looks highly transferable.

Second, that surface similarity repeatedly hides different underlying decisions once you ask what the person actually decides and what happens if they decide badly. "Manage risk" means a project risk register at HCA and Humana; at Wells Fargo it is a stated, separate accountability for Credit, Market, Financial-Crimes, Operational, and Regulatory Compliance risk programs; at Mass General Brigham it means serving as a Duty Officer during an infectious-disease surge. The verb is identical. The decision, the authority required, and the consequence of error are not.

Third, and cutting against a simple "nothing transfers" conclusion, direct same-industry experience is a hard gate in only 6 of 28 postings. It is preferred, ambiguous, or entirely absent in the other 22. Two postings state the tension outright in opposite directions: JPMorgan's Wealth Management PM requires 7+ years of financial services and lists project-management background as merely preferred, while Humana explicitly wants the candidate "comfortable working on new projects with limited knowledge/expertise of the content." Employers themselves disagree about whether the domain or the method is the real requirement.

The honest headline: some underlying delivery work appears comparable across these three industries, but domain knowledge, a handful of credential gates, and specific internal-context requirements are substantial enough that the postings do not support a blanket "your experience transfers" claim. They equally do not support "it does not." The transferable part is real and nameable; so are the barriers.

---

## SECTION 2: STRONGEST POSSIBLE YOUTUBE OPENING

The strongest evidence-supported opening is a same-word, different-job comparison the viewer can feel immediately.

Take one sentence that appears, in substance, in postings from all three industries: *"lead complex cross-functional programs from initiation through delivery, managing scope, schedule, risk, and stakeholders."* Cone Health wrote a version of it. Wells Fargo wrote a version of it. Amazon wrote a version of it. On paper these look like the same job.

Now put the risk clause under a microscope, because "risk" is where the three quietly diverge:

- At **Humana**, the Senior Project Manager runs a project risk register: if a risk is mishandled, a project slips.
- At **Wells Fargo**, every posting carries a standing accountability for "all applicable risk programs (Credit, Market, Financial Crimes, Operational, Regulatory Compliance)": if mishandled, the consequence is regulatory and financial, not a slipped timeline.
- At **Mass General Brigham's Center for Disaster Medicine**, the Senior Project Manager serves as a Duty Officer and helps run patient movement "during infectious disease surge events," with ICS/NIMS incident-command training required before or upon hire: if mishandled, the consequence is measured in patient care, not schedule.

Same word — "manage risk." Three different decisions, three different failure modes, three different bodies of knowledge you cannot fake. That is the honest hook: the résumé line transfers; the job underneath it may not. What actually travels is the muscle of running a plan and moving a group of people who don't report to you. What does not travel is knowing what "wrong" looks like in a new domain before it happens.

One concrete, defensible example to anchor the segment: the Humana posting is the clearest evidence for the optimistic reading (content knowledge explicitly not required), and the Wells Fargo standing risk-program clause is the clearest evidence for the cautious reading. Show both. They are from the same industry family, which makes the point sharper than any cross-industry contrast.

Do not oversell it into "roles are interchangeable." The data does not earn that (Section 12).

## SECTION 6: REAL CAPABILITY OVERLAP

These are the underlying work units that recur across all three industries where the decision and the consequence are close enough to call comparable within this sample. Each is grounded in postings from all three industries.

**1. End-to-end delivery ownership of a plan under a triple constraint.** Owning a plan from initiation to closure against scope, schedule, and budget, and being the single accountable party when it slips. Healthcare: HCA (H5) "single accountable party to define, plan, orchestrate, and deliver"; Humana (H10) "designs, communicates, and implements an operational plan... work is performed without direction." Finance: BofA (F2) "own and lead the end-to-end plan"; Capital One (F9) "design, develop, and manage project plans in a complex dynamic environment." Tech: Amazon (T2) "own your projects end-to-end"; Adobe (T5) "primary 'go to' person for project details." The decision (sequence work, allocate finite resources, decide what slips) and the consequence (a committed deliverable is late or over budget) are genuinely comparable across all three.

**2. Cross-functional stakeholder management without direct authority.** Moving a group of people who do not report to you toward a shared outcome. Healthcare: Amgen (H9) "influencing without authority and navigating within a matrix environment"; Highmark (H6) coordinates "a matrix group of employees and contractors." Finance: JPMorgan (F4) "cross-functional collaboration"; Capital One (F9) "influence at all levels." Tech: Microsoft (T3) "coordinate and ensure alignment across the business"; Google (T4) "drive alignment across engineering, product, data, and partner teams." This is the most uniformly worded capability in the sample and the decision underneath — how to sequence influence when you cannot direct — is comparable.

**3. Risk and issue identification with a maintained register and escalation path.** The *mechanics* (identify early, assess impact, mitigate, escalate) recur everywhere: Cone Health (H4), MGB (H8), Wells Fargo (F1), Allstate (F6), Oracle (T7 "create risk management plans and maintain risk register"), Amazon (T2 "de-risk situations"). Note the sharp limit: the *mechanics* of risk management transfer; the *content* of what counts as a risk does not (see Section 7).

**4. Executive-level status communication and governance.** Building and running the reporting cadence that keeps senior leaders deciding. Healthcare: Humana (H10) "executive-level communications"; HCA (H5) "routinely briefs key stakeholders." Finance: Wells Fargo (F1) "status reporting... executive reporting"; BofA (F2) "present... to executive audiences." Tech: Google (T4) "lead a governance structure that drives effective executive decision-making"; Salesforce (T1) "leadership readouts covering risks, metrics, and decisions." Comparable decision: what to escalate, to whom, and when.

**5. Process/tooling discipline (SDLC, stage-gate, Agile/Waterfall).** The delivery-methodology layer recurs in all three: CVS (H2) "stage gate quality and SDLC control guidelines"; Wells Fargo (F1) "SAFe/Agile full lifecycle"; Salesforce (T1) "hybrid delivery model (Waterfall and Agile)." This is method knowledge, largely readable/learnable (Section 10).

The defensible claim from this section: an experienced senior IC delivery professional can reasonably say these five capabilities — plan ownership, influence without authority, the mechanics of risk/issue management, executive governance communication, and delivery-method discipline — are demonstrably the shared spine of this role family across all three sampled industries.

## SECTION 7: APPARENT OVERLAP THAT FAILED THE TEST

This is the section the research most strongly supports, and the heart of the video. In each case the noun or verb matches but the decision and consequence diverge.

**"Manage risk" — three different jobs.** Covered in Section 2. Humana (H10): project risk register; failure = slipped project. Wells Fargo (F1): standing accountability for Credit/Market/Financial-Crimes/Operational/Regulatory-Compliance risk programs; failure = regulatory/financial exposure. MGB (H8): Duty Officer during infectious-disease surge, ICS/NIMS required; failure = patient-care consequence. Same words, three incomparable decisions. This is the cleanest failed-overlap in the sample.

**"Governance" — auditable control vs. meeting cadence.** JPMorgan (F4): "maintain a robust and auditable governance process" and "ensure all projects are completed in accordance with all Risk, Control, and other relevant firm-wide guidelines" — governance here means creating an audit trail that satisfies a regulated control environment; a bad decision has compliance consequences. Google (T4): "lead a governance structure that drives effective executive decision-making... exposes and mitigates dependencies" — governance here means a decision-forum cadence; a bad decision slows delivery. Both say "governance." One is a control artifact for regulators; the other is a coordination ritual for executives.

**"Stakeholder management" — regulators/desks vs. engineers/product.** BofA (F2): stakeholders are "Global Markets Trading Desks, Technology, Operations, Finance, Risk," and success requires knowing "the processes, procedures and policies that govern change... at Bank of America." Microsoft (T3): stakeholders are "Software Engineering, Data Scientists, Designers, and SMEs" and success requires designing "complex technical solutions across feature areas." The verb "manage stakeholders" is identical; the knowledge required to be credible with a trading desk vs. a data-science team is not interchangeable.

**"Financial management" — project budget vs. revenue/margin ownership.** Most IC postings mean "track the project budget" (H5 "validates financial forecasts"; T1 "budget forecasting"). But Cisco's Customer Program Manager (T8) means "manage forecasting, invoicing, revenue, cost, margin, and accurate reporting" — a P&L-adjacent decision most of the sample does not carry. "Owns financials" on a résumé could mean either; the underlying authority differs by an order of magnitude.

**"Regulated industry experience" — sometimes a real gate, sometimes deliberately broad.** Medtronic (H7) requires "experience in a regulated industry" but explicitly lists "medical devices, aerospace, automotive" — the employer is telling you the transferable unit is regulated-hardware lifecycle discipline, not healthcare. Contrast Wells Fargo (F1), where "financial Industry experience" is a hard, specific gate. The phrase "regulated industry" therefore fails as a single category: one employer uses it to *invite* cross-industry movement, another to *block* it.

**"Product/technical program management" — degree-gated engineering fluency vs. not.** Gilead (H3) requires an engineering degree; Oracle (T7) requires a CS/IT/Telecom degree and 10+ yrs in cloud infrastructure; but Amazon (T2) and Microsoft (T3) gate on years of "working with engineering teams" with no degree-field or credential requirement. "Technical program manager" describes both, but one path requires provable engineering domain depth and the other requires demonstrated collaboration with engineers.

## SECTION 8: WHAT DOES NOT TRAVEL CLEANLY

Grouped as requested.

**Domain knowledge.** The specific subject matter is the least transferable element and is almost always industry-bound: Medicaid provider engagement (H1), underwriting/sales workflows (H6), NPI/EOL device lifecycle (H7), infectious-disease patient movement (H8), Global Markets products and derivatives (F2, F4), payments/ACH/authentication rails (F3, F7), card/personal-loan operations (F10), cloud compute/virtualization/GPU architecture (T7), networking routing/switching (T6, T8). None of these is described as learnable-on-arrival except where a posting says so explicitly (H10 Humana, F10 Capital One "building your business knowledge").

**Credentials/licenses.** No occupational *license* (in the legal sense) appears in any retained posting — this is not a licensed role family. What appears are professional certifications. PMP is a HARD gate in 4 of 28 (H4 Cone Health, F9 Capital One, T1 Salesforce, T8 Cisco); Cone Health additionally requires a Lean Six Sigma belt as HARD. Everywhere else PMP is preferred or unmentioned. Whether any of these is *legally* required: NOT DETERMINABLE FROM POSTING in every case — none is a statutory license. Two postings add hard eligibility gates that are not credentials but do not travel: Highmark (H6) "must be US Citizen (contractual/access)"; several tech postings state no visa sponsorship (T3 Microsoft, and Fidelity F8) or unrestricted-work-authorization requirements (H7 Medtronic).

**Tools/platforms.** Two tiers. Broadly shared and low-barrier: MS Office/G Suite, Jira, Confluence, Smartsheet, MS Project — required or preferred across all three industries; these travel. Industry- or employer-flavored: Planisware/OnePager (pharma portfolio tools, H9), SAP/Oracle/Agile/Arena PLM (device manufacturing, H7), Workday/Benefex/Equity Edge/Concur (Salesforce internal systems, T1), Cisco Services Portfolio tooling (T6, T8). **Stated direct industry experience.** Hard gate in 6 of 28: H1 (provider engagement), H2 (healthcare PM), F1 (financial industry), F2 (Global Markets/comparable FS), F5 (financial services), T6 (Cisco networking portfolio). Preferred or ambiguous in ~13. Absent in the rest. So same-industry experience is demanded outright in under a quarter of the sample — the single most important number against a blanket "you must already be in the industry" claim, and equally against "industry never matters."

**Relationships/context (where visible).** Employer-internal knowledge that an outsider cannot reproduce by studying: BofA's "Enterprise Change Management Policies and Standards" (F2) and Fidelity's "end-to-end process of Fidelity Health Care Client Delivery" (F8). These are the clearest GATED items (Section 10) — they require being inside the organization.

## SECTION 10: READABLE / OBSERVABLE / GATED CONTEXT

Classifying each recurring domain requirement by how the knowledge appears to be acquired. Judgment calls; original wording preserved in Section 4.

**READABLE (learnable through study/documentation/instruction):**
- Delivery methodologies — SDLC, stage-gate, Agile/SAFe/Scrum, PMBOK (H2, F1, T1, T5). These are the basis of the certifications themselves, so by definition largely readable.
- Broadly shared tools — Jira, Confluence, MS Project, MS Office (across all industries).
- Regulatory *frameworks* as published standards — ICH/GCP concepts, HIPAA privacy rules, CMS Stars methodology — are documented and studyable in principle (adjacent to H1, H6, H8).

**OBSERVABLE (requires exposure to the environment to understand how the work actually operates):**
- "How a trading desk actually makes decisions" behind BofA's (F2) stakeholder requirement — you can read about Global Markets products, but the working rhythm is learned by being near it.
- NPI/EOL "IBP process" cadence at Medtronic (H7) — the process is documentable, but its real operation is observed.
- Provider-engagement operational reality behind CVS (H1) — Medicaid rules are readable; how provider onboarding actually breaks is observed.
- Payments rails in practice (F3, F7) — the protocols are documented; the failure modes are observed.

**GATED (requires access, authority, supervised practice, or being inside — an outsider generally cannot reproduce by reading):**
- Employer-internal process/policy: BofA "Enterprise Change Management Policies and Standards" (F2); Fidelity "end-to-end process of Fidelity Health Care Client Delivery" (F8). You must be inside the organization.
- Incident-command *practice* at MGB (H8) — ICS/NIMS courses are readable (that part is READABLE), but serving as a Duty Officer during a real surge is supervised, access-gated practice.
- Standing enterprise risk-program accountability at Wells Fargo (F1) — exercising authority over Credit/Market/Compliance risk decisions requires being granted that authority.
- US-citizenship/clearance-gated work (Highmark H6) — a hard access gate no study can substitute for.

Pattern: the *transferable spine* (Section 6) sits almost entirely in READABLE + the universally-shared-tool layer. The *non-transferable* content (Sections 7–8) concentrates in OBSERVABLE and GATED. That mapping is the mechanism behind the mixed finding: what travels is what can be studied or is method; what does not travel is what must be seen or accessed from inside.

- The READABLE/OBSERVABLE/GATED placement of the employer-internal items (F2, F8) as GATED was unambiguous.

**Judgments that remained genuinely difficult:**
- **H2 (CVS) and T4 (Google) — domain fused into an experience number.** "7-10 years' experience in HealthCare Project management" (H2) and "8 years of infrastructure work experience" (T4) blend EXP and DOM. I coded them as HARD experience with an embedded domain gate, but a defensible alternative is to treat them as pure domain gates. This affects the industry-experience HARD count (I placed H2 in HARD, T4 in NOT-STATED-industry because "infrastructure" is a technical domain, not an industry). Reasonable coders could move T4. Flagged, not hidden.
- **F7 (Mastercard) — no Required/Preferred headers at all.** The posting uses an "ALL ABOUT YOU" list. I classified payments knowledge as PREFERRED (it says "huge plus") and the engineering degree as a floor, but the hard/preferred line is genuinely AMBIGUOUS by the employer's own formatting. This is the single least reliable row for the hard/preferred counts.
- **F8 (Fidelity) — "experienced knowledge in the end-to-end process of Fidelity Health Care Client Delivery."** Listed among skills, not under a Required header, and the years/education line is explicitly "preferred." Whether the internal-process knowledge is a hard gate or an aspiration is not resolvable from the text. I treated it as GATED-in-kind but AMBIGUOUS-in-force.
- **Seniority of H8 (MGB) and F10 (Capital One).** H8 pays $62–91K (low for "senior") and F10 accepts HS/GED + 2 years. Both fit the role family on *work described* (independent ownership, no core line management), which the brief says to prioritize over title and pay. I retained both on that rule, but a stricter seniority screen would drop them, which would pull healthcare and finance to 9 each.

**Disagreements not smoothed over:** the industry-experience HARD count could reasonably be 5, 6, or 7 of 28 depending on how H2/T4/F7 are resolved. I report 6 as the central estimate and show the movement here rather than presenting 6 as exact.

## SECTION 12: RESEARCH LIMITATIONS

What this sample cannot establish, restated plainly and kept visible:

- It cannot show that any two of these roles are interchangeable, or that a person could move between them.
- It cannot show that a hiring manager would waive any stated requirement, or that an adjacent-experience candidate would be interviewed, let alone hired.
- It cannot show that the written posting reflects the actual job; job descriptions are aspirational and often boilerplate (several here carried copy-paste "people leader" or "risk program" blocks).
- It cannot show that someone would succeed after moving industries, or that every part of prior experience transfers.
- It says nothing about compensation trajectories or promotion outcomes.
- It is not representative of any industry or of the US labor market. 28 postings, chosen for accessibility and codability, are a convenience sample. Do not extrapolate to "healthcare hiring" or "tech PMs" in general.
- Every count is a count *within these 28 postings*. "6 of 28 gate on industry experience" is a fact about this sample, not an estimate of any population rate.
- Several postings are dated 2025 or early 2026, or are captured from ATS mirrors rather than live employer pages; the text was verified on the collection date but roles may have changed or closed.

The strongest conclusion the evidence earns is exactly the one the brief anticipated: *some underlying delivery work appears comparable across these three industries, but the differences in domain, a few credential gates, and internal-context requirements are substantial enough that the postings do not support a simple transferability claim.* The evidence does earn a sharper sub-claim — that the shared, transferable part is specifically nameable (the five capabilities in Section 6) and that the non-transferable part maps onto knowledge that must be observed or accessed from inside (Section 10). It does not earn anything stronger than that.

## SECTION 13: POSSIBLE VIDEO PACKAGING

**A. Strongest evidence-supported title.**
Keep the planning title, lightly sharpened: **"Which Parts of Your Experience Actually Transfer to Another Industry?"** It is honest to a mixed finding and promises a distinction rather than a verdict.
A numerical/past-tense title is CONDITIONAL and, in my judgment, *earns its place here* because the coded work was actually done — but only if stated truthfully as **"I Read 28 Job Descriptions Across 3 Industries. Here's What Transfers."** The number must be 28, never 30. Do not use "30." If you would rather not foreground a number that fell short of target, use the sharpened question title instead. I would lean to the question title, because the finding is a distinction, not a count.

**B. Strongest thumbnail text.**
Keep **"WHAT REALLY TRANSFERS?"** — it fits the evidence exactly. A strong alternative that previews the actual finding: **"SAME WORDS. DIFFERENT JOB."**

**C. Strongest first-30-second hook (from the data only).**
"Three job postings. A hospital, a bank, and Amazon. All three say the same thing: *lead complex programs, manage scope, schedule, risk, and stakeholders.* On paper, one person could do all three. So I looked at what the word 'risk' actually means in each. At the hospital's disaster-medicine team, managing risk means being the on-call officer moving patients during an infectious-disease surge. At the bank, it means standing accountability for financial-crimes and regulatory-compliance programs. On the tech team, it means a project might slip. Same word. Three completely different jobs — and three different kinds of 'wrong' you can only recognize if you've been there. So the real question isn't whether your experience transfers. It's *which part.*"

**D. Three-to-five findings worth teaching.**
1. The transferable spine is real and nameable: plan ownership, influence without authority, risk/issue *mechanics*, executive governance communication, delivery-method discipline (Section 6). Teach these as the things a career-changer can legitimately claim.
2. "Same word, different decision" — the risk / governance / stakeholder / financials examples (Section 7). This is the memorable core.
3. Domain knowledge splits into READABLE, OBSERVABLE, and GATED — and only the readable/tool layer travels freely (Section 10). This gives viewers a way to audit their own experience.
4. Employers themselves disagree: within one industry, Humana says content knowledge isn't required while JPMorgan gates on the industry (Sections 1, 2). Transferability is partly a hiring-philosophy choice, not a law of the work.
5. Direct same-industry experience is a hard gate in only ~6 of 28 postings (Section 9). The door is open more often than "you need to already be in the industry" folklore suggests — but the credential and internal-context gates are real where they appear.

**E. Findings that should NOT be used because the evidence is too weak.**
- Do not claim any percentage of the labor market, or generalize to "healthcare/finance/tech hiring." The sample is 28 convenience-selected postings (Section 12).
- Do not present the PM-certification or industry-experience counts as precise; the hard/preferred line is format-dependent and moves by a point or two (Section 11). Say "in this sample, roughly…" or give the exact fraction with the denominator.
- Do not use the technology cell as if it were a full 10 — it is 8; if you cite tech specifically, say 8.
- Do not imply anyone would be hired across industries, or that a stated requirement would be waived — the postings cannot show that (Section 12).
- Avoid a numerical title unless you are comfortable saying "28" on screen; never round it to 30 for performance.
